How the Sonderline social content system works

This is a production system for Sonderline's social content. A team member writes a brief. Claude drafts a spec from the approved copy library. A layout engine renders every format to the brand grid, and a checker tests each output against the brand guide. Named people then approve the result before it is exported.

1 · Why start with social content

Social content has the most volume and the most repetition

Volume

Each message is produced in four or more formats (4:5 feed, 1:1 square, 1.91:1 link card and 9:16 story), often as a carousel as well, for the product, brand, regional and recruiting teams. Social is the content type the team produces most.

Where the time goes

Most production time is spent adapting finished work: resizing, re-cropping and re-setting type for each format, checking contrast and copy, and fixing mistakes found in review.

The brand rules can be automated

The Sonderline guide is precise. It specifies a fixed grid, five colours, vermilion at about 3% of a layout, a contrast minimum of 4.5:1 and supplied logos with h/2 clearspace. These rules are easy to break by hand and straightforward to check in code.

Claim risk

Pulse has not launched yet, and the guide says not to invent capabilities, performance numbers or testimonials. With many people producing posts quickly, unapproved claims are the biggest brand risk in social content.

2 · How the system works

Seven steps that share one set of brand rules

Claude is used for the language and selection decisions. Code handles anything that has to be exact, such as layout, contrast and logo placement. People make the decisions that need judgment or accountability. Every step reads the same rules files, so each rule is defined once.

01

Brief

The requester states the goal, audience and channels.

Prototype: Studio brief panel
Production: Asana, Jira or Airtable form

02

Draft spec

Claude chooses approved lines, a layout, a photo, a crop and the formats, and drafts the caption. If the brief needs copy the library doesn't have, Claude flags it instead of writing it.

Claude API (Opus 5.5), structured JSON output

03

Compose

The engine renders every format from the spec: grid, type fitting, line breaks and a scrim set to meet the contrast minimum.

Canvas renderer in the browser and in headless Chromium

04

Check

Thirteen rules from the guide are checked on the layout geometry and the rendered pixels. Each check passes, goes to a named reviewer, or blocks approval.

Validator, the same code in every tool

05

Review

The designer and brand lead review every post. Product marketing reviews any new copy. Reviewers can still change the crop, layout or copy.

Prototype: Studio approvals
Production: Figma or Frame.io

06

Deliver

The images or video, caption, alt text, spec, check report and approval record are exported together.

Prototype: zip or MP4 export
Production: DAM and scheduler

07

Update the rules

New approved lines are added to the library. Overrides that keep recurring become new layout rules.

4 · Quality control and approvals

Automated checks, and the decisions people make

Checked automatically on every output

Approved on-image copy (p13) · measured text contrast of at least 4.5:1 (p06) · approved colour pairings (p06) · brand palette only (p03) · vermilion at or below 5% of pixels (p04) · Inter Tight 400 and 700 (p05) · text fit and collisions (p07) · minimum type size (p05) · supplied logo, undistorted, with clearspace (p02) · black footer (p07) · story safe zones · photo source and enlargement (p08) · flagged use of assumed assets · caption claims and alt text (p13). Video scenes are checked at the start, middle and end of each move.

An output I would refine before delivery

A regional team asked for a post about escalations and wrote in the brief that "Pulse cuts escalation response times by 40%." The three versions are on the deliverables page.

  1. Version 1, first draft (blocked). The white headline on bright metal measured 1.39:1. The post caption repeats the brief's "cuts escalation response times by 40%", which is an unapproved performance number, so it is blocked. The lead line on the image, "Resolve escalations faster across every store", makes the same claim without a number; it is not in the library, so it goes to product marketing. Nobody can approve the post until these are fixed.
  2. Version 2, automatic fix (passes the checks). Product marketing replaces the copy with approved lines, and the engine adds a 50% scrim to meet the contrast minimum. Every check passes, but the scrim makes the metal look flat and grey, so I would not publish it.
  3. Version 3, designer refined (approved). The designer re-crops each format so the photo's own shadow sits behind the headline. The feed post needs a 30% scrim and the link card needs none, and the texture of the metal is kept. The designer makes this change and the brand lead approves it. Product marketing approved the copy change in version 2.

The checks make problems visible and quick to fix. Decisions about craft, like the crop in version 3, are made by the designer.

Limitations and failure points

  • Limited photography. Two supplied photos limit variety. A real photo library would need tags for photo type, safe crops and dark areas. Generated footage also needs rules for likeness, provenance and disclosure.
  • Implied claims. Words like "faster" or "smarter" contain no numbers, so the blocked patterns can't catch them. They are caught because they are new copy, which always goes to a person. That review step needs to stay in place.
  • Weak drafts. Claude may choose a flat line or a poor crop. The checks limit the damage and the designer can override. If the API is unavailable, the offline drafter and hand-edited specs still work.
  • Measured contrast. Contrast is measured on the pixels under each text block. A busy texture can still look worse than its measurement, so the designer has to judge it.
  • Fixed layouts. A new campaign look needs new layout families. Each one takes a designer and a creative technologist about one to two days, and can then be reused.
  • Prototype shortcuts. The API key is entered in the browser, approvals are typed names instead of single sign-on, and a zip file stands in for a DAM.

6 · Rolling it out to the team

A small pilot with designers in control

Weeks 1–2: baseline

Time 20 real social requests from start to finish. Confirm the copy library with product marketing and the rules with the brand lead, and fill gaps such as the flow graphics.

Weeks 3–6: pilot

One social team uses the Studio for real requests, with full override rights for designers. Review overrides weekly, and turn repeated overrides into rules or new layouts.

Weeks 7–12: expand

Connect intake and the DAM, then add presentations and video on the same engine and rules. Train the wider team in short sessions using their own briefs.

Ownership

The brand lead owns the rules and the library. Product marketing owns claims. A creative technologist maintains the layouts and the engine. Overrides and failures are reviewed monthly.

7 · Expected impact

Time saved, consistency and fewer review rounds

Time per post set (estimate)

By hand, about three to five hours to lay out four formats, resize, check copy and run QA. With the system, about 30 to 60 minutes to brief, review and approve.

At volume (estimate)

At 50 post sets a month, about 120 to 200 designer hours a month would move from adaptation work to concept work. The same team would produce more work.

Quality

Off-palette colours, low contrast, distorted logos and unapproved claims are flagged before review, so reviewers can spend their time on creative judgment.

Measuring it

Measure a baseline first, then track time to publish, review rounds per asset, override rate and check failure rate. The estimates here should be replaced with Sonderline's own figures.

8 · Assumptions and sources

Assumptions

  • Brand Guide 8.0 is the source of truth. Where the kit's notes differ from the guide (for example, a mockup note describing a full vermilion panel), I followed the guide.
  • Flow graphics were listed in the brief but were not in the kit. I designed one, called Contour, based on the stroke of the S symbol: fine bands that step together, with one vermilion line. It is used on carousel covers and closing frames, on video title and end cards, and as the transition between video scenes. Until Sonderline confirms it or supplies its own, any output that uses it goes to the brand lead.
  • Approved copy is the copy on page 13 of the guide plus the lines used in the guide's examples.
  • Format sizes and story safe zones (top 250px, bottom 340px) follow current platform guidance.
  • Volumes and time savings are estimates until a baseline is measured.
